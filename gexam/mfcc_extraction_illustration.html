How speech becomes MFCCs

MFCC extraction turns short slices of a speech waveform into compact vectors that summarize the shape of their frequency content.

Big picture: One audio recording becomes many short frames. Each frame is converted from a waveform into a spectrum, summarized on the Mel scale, compressed with a logarithm and DCT, and represented by a small vector of coefficients.

MFCC sequence
1

Start with the waveform

Sound pressure recorded over time.

Speech changes continuously. The analysis uses short windows (often around 20–30 ms) and advances by a smaller hop, so adjacent frames overlap.

2

Window each frame, then take its spectrum

Describe which frequencies are present in that brief slice.

The window (commonly a Hamming window) tapers the frame edges. A Fourier transform then estimates energy at different frequencies; MFCCs use the magnitude or power spectrum.

3

Summarize energy with Mel filters

Combine nearby frequency bins into perceptually spaced bands.

Each triangle measures a weighted sum of spectrum energy in its band. The Mel scale places more filters in lower frequencies and fewer, wider-spaced filters at higher frequencies.

4

Take log energies, then apply the DCT

Convert filter-bank energies into a compact cepstral description.

The logarithm compresses energy differences. The discrete cosine transform (DCT) expresses the log Mel spectrum as coefficients; many systems keep only the first several coefficients, sometimes excluding or treating the overall-energy coefficient separately.

Final output: a sequence of feature vectors

audio → frames → spectrum → Mel-band energies → log → DCT → MFCCs

Each frame produces a vector such as [c₀, c₁, c₂, …]. Stacking the frame vectors gives a time sequence of features that a speech model can process. MFCC extraction summarizes each short frame; it does not by itself recognize words or model the sentence.
