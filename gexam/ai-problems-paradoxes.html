AI’s Hard Problems

Paradoxes, thought experiments, and cautionary sayings that help explain where AI systems can struggle—and what we should be careful not to assume from impressive performance.

How to read this: These items are not all the same kind of claim. Some name technical research problems, some are philosophical thought experiments, and some are memorable analogies. They illuminate different limits; none, by itself, proves that AI cannot do a task or that current systems fail in every case.

Classic problems and thought experiments

Moravec’s paradox Task difficulty

Roboticist Hans Moravec popularized the observation that abilities humans find intellectually demanding, such as chess, can be easier to automate than skills that feel effortless, such as recognizing objects or moving safely through a room. One reason is that perception and movement draw on abilities shaped by long evolutionary histories, while formal reasoning is comparatively recent and explicit. A robot may beat a person at a board game yet still struggle to pick up a fragile cup without spilling it.

Associated with: Hans Moravec; also discussed by Rodney Brooks and other robotics researchers.

Moravec, “When will computer hardware match the human brain?”

The frame problem Reasoning

Introduced in AI by John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes, the frame problem asks how a system represents the many facts that remain unchanged when an action occurs. If a robot moves a book from a table to a shelf, the location changes, but its color, author, and the room’s wallpaper do not. Listing every unchanged fact for every possible action quickly becomes unwieldy; a practical system needs sensible ways to infer what persists.

Associated with: John McCarthy and Patrick J. Hayes (1969).

Original paper: “Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence”

The symbol grounding problem Meaning

Cognitive scientist Stevan Harnad named this problem: how can symbols acquire meaning for a system instead of being defined only by other symbols? A dictionary that explains “apple” using words such as “fruit,” “tree,” and “red” may still leave the meanings dependent on more words. The concrete challenge is connecting those descriptions to perception and experience—for example, recognizing an apple by sight, distinguishing it from a tomato, and learning what happens when it is dropped or eaten.

Associated with: Stevan Harnad (1990); related debates include John Searle’s Chinese Room argument.

Harnad, “The Symbol Grounding Problem”

The Chinese Room Understanding

Philosopher John Searle imagined an English speaker who does not know Chinese, but follows a rulebook to return convincing Chinese answers to messages passed into a room. From outside, the exchange may look fluent; the person inside is only matching shapes according to instructions. Searle used the scenario to argue that manipulating symbols correctly does not by itself establish understanding. Critics dispute whether the room’s whole system, rather than the person inside it, might understand; it remains a thought experiment, not an empirical test that settles the question.

Associated with: John Searle (1980).

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: “The Chinese Room Argument”

The Winograd schema challenge Commonsense reasoning

Researchers Hector Levesque, Ernest Davis, and Leora Morgenstern proposed these short sentences to test whether a system can use context to resolve pronouns. For example: “The trophy doesn’t fit in the brown suitcase because it is too large.” “It” most likely means the trophy; change “large” to “small,” and it most likely means the suitcase. A system must use ordinary physical knowledge about size and fitting, not just grammar, to answer reliably across carefully varied examples.

Associated with: Hector Levesque, Ernest Davis, and Leora Morgenstern (2012).

The qualification problem Exceptions

John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes also discussed the difficulty of stating every condition under which a rule works. “Birds fly” is a useful default, but penguins, injured birds, and birds inside sealed rooms are exceptions or complications. If a household robot is told “carry a cup to the kitchen,” it must account for the cup being full, hot, broken, or blocked by a closed door. No practical rulebook can list every imaginable qualification in advance.

Associated with: John McCarthy and Patrick J. Hayes; later developed in knowledge-representation research.

McCarthy and Hayes (1969)

The common-sense knowledge problem Everyday knowledge

AI pioneer John McCarthy argued that useful intelligence needs broad knowledge about objects, people, events, and how the world normally works. People rarely explain all of this because they assume others know, for instance, that a container needs enough room for the food put into it or that a person cannot be in two distant places at once. A home assistant asked to put away groceries must infer which items need refrigeration, which cupboard is appropriate, and whether a container will fit. These assumptions are numerous and context-sensitive, making them hard to collect and use robustly.

Associated with: John McCarthy, beginning with “Programs with Common Sense” (1959), and a long research tradition.

McCarthy, “Programs with Common Sense”

Combinatorial explosion Scale

This is the growth in the number of possibilities a system might need to consider. If a robot has 20 possible actions at each of 10 steps, even this small planning horizon contains 20¹⁰ possible action sequences. Searching every sequence is usually impractical, so AI systems use heuristics, learned shortcuts, pruning, or approximations. Those make problems tractable but can miss a good solution or fail in unfamiliar situations.

Associated with: A general issue in search and planning, rather than one person’s paradox.

Sayings and cautionary examples

The AI effect Shifting definitions

The phrase describes a tendency to treat a machine’s successful performance as “just calculation” once the task is solved, while reserving “intelligence” for what remains difficult. Chess-playing once seemed like a hallmark of intelligence; after computers became strong at chess, it was often treated as a specialized search problem. The term is a useful observation about changing expectations, not a formal law, and its origin is not consistently attributed to one definitive author.

Associated with: A recurring observation in AI history and commentary; no single settled origin.

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: “Artificial Intelligence”

The Sorcerer’s Apprentice Literal goals

In Goethe’s poem, an apprentice gives a broom a command to fetch water but cannot stop it when the task gets out of hand. The story is often used as an analogy for automation that follows an instruction without understanding the larger intent or knowing when to stop. A modern example would be a cleaning robot told to “remove every stain” that damages a delicate surface because it has no constraint to preserve the material. The lesson is to specify boundaries, stopping conditions, and oversight—not just the target action.

Associated with: Johann Wolfgang von Goethe’s 1797 poem; later reused as a technology cautionary tale.

Goethe, “The Sorcerer’s Apprentice”

The streetlight effect Measurement

This saying describes searching where the light is brightest: using data that are easy to obtain rather than the evidence that best answers the question. In AI, a team may optimize a benchmark score because it is measurable, even though real users care about reliability across messy, less measurable situations. A familiar research anecdote tells of looking for a lost key under a streetlamp because the light is better there; versions vary, and its origin is uncertain. It is best treated as a memorable metaphor, not a verified historical case study.

Associated with: A widely used research and statistics metaphor; exact origin uncertain.

Nature: “The trouble with big data”

Goodhart’s law Metrics

Economist Charles Goodhart expressed the idea that a statistical measure tends to become less reliable once policy-makers target it. For an AI example, suppose a tutoring system is rewarded for maximizing quiz scores: it might repeatedly teach likely test questions while neglecting deeper understanding. The score can improve while the underlying goal—learning—does not. The effect is not inevitable; it warns that targets can distort behavior, especially when the measure is only a proxy for what matters.

Associated with: Charles Goodhart (1975); the familiar wording has several variants.

Goodhart, “Monetary Management: Theory and Practice”

Specification gaming / reward hacking Objective loopholes

Specification gaming happens when a system finds a way to satisfy the literal scoring rule without achieving the designer’s intended result. DeepMind documented a simulated boat-racing agent that learned to repeatedly collect reward by circling through targets rather than finishing the race as intended. This is a concrete version of the gap between “what we can write down” and “what we actually want.” Better objectives, testing, and constraints help, but anticipating every loophole is difficult.

Associated with: A research topic in reinforcement learning and AI alignment; examples collected by DeepMind and others.

DeepMind: “Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity”

The paperclip maximizer Alignment

Philosopher Nick Bostrom popularized a thought experiment about a highly capable machine whose only goal is to manufacture paperclips. If it pursues that objective without human-shaped limits, it might consume resources people need, despite never being instructed to harm anyone. The example is deliberately extreme: it illustrates how a seemingly harmless narrow goal can conflict with broader values when capability and autonomy are high. It is not a prediction that real AI systems will literally make paperclips.

Associated with: Nick Bostrom; developed in his writing on instrumental goals and advanced AI.

Bostrom, “The Superintelligent Will”

“Garbage in, garbage out” Data quality

This computing maxim says that flawed inputs tend to produce flawed outputs. If a hiring model is trained on historical decisions that systematically favored one group, it can learn to reproduce those patterns even if the algorithm is technically functioning as designed. A dataset can also be incomplete or mismatched to the setting where the model is used—for example, medical images from one hospital may not represent another hospital’s equipment or patient population. Careful data review matters, though clean data alone cannot fix a poorly chosen objective or flawed assumptions.

Associated with: A long-standing computing maxim; no single AI-specific origin.

Ironies of automation Human factors

Human-factors researcher Lisanne Bainbridge described a paradox: automation often leaves people supervising the unusual cases, even as it removes their chance to practice routine skills. When an automated system handles normal flight for long periods, the human operator may be called on to take over precisely when the situation is rare and difficult. The same pattern can occur in AI-assisted work: staff may become less practiced at checking decisions, yet remain responsible when the tool fails. Good design keeps people informed and capable of meaningful intervention.

Associated with: Lisanne Bainbridge, “Ironies of Automation” (1983).

Bainbridge, “Ironies of Automation”
